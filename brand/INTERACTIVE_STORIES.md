## Lumo — The Gate of Sixes

**Heartlight:** Questlight  
**Id:** `gate-of-sixes`

> I rushed. Your turn to choose our strategy.

The gate shows four constellations, and each one holds six stars. How many stars is that?

### Choose a strategy

- **Count in sixes.** Start at six, then add another six, and keep the beat: six, twelve, eighteen…
- **Draw the groups.** Four little clusters. Count one cluster, then the next, until the sky is counted.
- **Split six into five and one.** Each constellation is five stars and one more. Gather the fives, then the ones.

### Reveal the explanation

All three paths land on twenty-four. Counting in sixes goes 6, 12, 18, 24. Four drawn groups of six are the same stars. Splitting them makes four fives (20) and four ones (4), and 20 + 4 is 24. Questlight’s glow is for checking the work together — not for pretending Lumo knew it the first time.

### Try this next

Find four groups of six small things, like buttons, blocks or pebbles. Count them in sixes, as groups, and as fives-and-ones. See the same total arrive three ways.

### Grown-up note

Lumo admits a rushed guess, then hands the strategy to the child. Praise the check, not the speed. Offer a pause if the counting feels heavy. This is a story about cooperating with a problem, not evidence of better marks.

### Writing prompt

Which strategy would you try first, and why?

## Lumo — The Save-Point Bridge

**Heartlight:** Questlight  
**Id:** `save-point`

> Save point. We can stop, and the quest will wait.

The star bridge is longer than it looked. What should the crew do?

### Choose a strategy

- **Rest at the lantern.** Lumo marks the plank with Questlight. Nothing unspools while you sit.
- **Three more steps, then rest.** A short plan, then the same lantern. The bridge does not punish a pause.
- **Stop right now.** Lumo nods. “Player one. Crew first. That includes you being finished.”

### Reveal the explanation

A save point is a promise that the place will still be there. Leaving a quest is not losing it. Lumo used to think heroes never sat down. The bridge only opens for a crew that can choose to stop.

### Try this next

Invent a save-point signal for a real task: a bookmark, a sticker, or a word like “lantern”. Use it once this week when you want to pause without calling it quitting.

### Grown-up note

The story welcomes a stop. If a child says they are done, treat that as information, not a hurdle to coach them past. It does not claim that breaks improve results.

### Writing prompt

What would your save point look like?

## Lumo — The Rumour Rune

**Heartlight:** Questlight  
**Id:** `rumour-rune`

> That sounds huge. Let’s read the carving before we believe it.

A whisper says the portal opens only for the fastest player. What should Lumo do?

### Choose a strategy

- **Read the rune together.** The carving is the source. The whisper is somebody’s version of it.
- **Ask what a friend actually saw.** Nori remembers a different word. Two memories are a reason to look again.
- **Try one slow step and watch.** A careful test. If the portal stays shut, the rumour still might be wrong.

### Reveal the explanation

The rune says “Enter together.” The rumour dropped the last word and added “fastest”. Checking the carving changes the plan: the crew walks in side by side. A exciting story is not the same as the instruction.

### Try this next

Play missing-word. One person says a short rule and leaves a word out. The other finds what changed. Swap.

### Grown-up note

This shows the difference between a retelling and a source. It is not a safety guarantee, and it does not teach a child to handle real rumours alone. Stay nearby for anything that matters.

### Writing prompt

What would you want to check before you believed a big rumour?

## Sprout — The Upside-Down Map

**Heartlight:** Traillight  
**Id:** `upside-down-map`

> I announced the plan. Then I noticed the map was upside down. Second try?

Sprout’s leaf-map was the wrong way up. Which move now?

### Choose a strategy

- **Turn it and look again.** The leaf arrows point the other way once the map is the right way up.
- **Test the first path slowly.** One careful step. If the moss thins out, come back. That is still a plan.
- **Ask what Nori notices.** A second pair of eyes. Sprout does not have to spot everything alone.

### Reveal the explanation

The trail marks were leaves pointing toward the moss gate, not away from it. Sprout’s medal is for the second look. Traillight brightens when a plan gets repaired, not when it was perfect the first time.

### Try this next

Turn a drawing upside down and find one thing you missed. Then turn it back and tell someone what the second look found.

### Grown-up note

Sprout celebrates a thoughtful retry instead of hiding the mix-up. Skip “you should have looked first.” The repair is the point. This does not promise fewer mistakes at school.

### Writing prompt

Tell about a second try that helped — or invent one.

## Sprout — Two Paths, One Puddle

**Heartlight:** Traillight  
**Id:** `two-paths`

> New route. Same brave little feet. Which pattern first?

A puddle sits across the path. How do we cross?

### Choose a strategy

- **Stepping stones.** Look at each stone before a boot goes down. Slippery is information.
- **A leaf boat.** Small, silly, and only for a very shallow puddle. Name it if you like.
- **The drier pebbles.** Nori points at a line of pebbles easy to miss. You may change your mind.

### Reveal the explanation

Every choice can be a kind plan. The drier pebbles were there the whole time. Traillight marks the way you pick, and it does not sulk if you switch. Noticing is allowed to happen late.

### Try this next

On paper, draw a puddle and two ways around it. Circle the way that feels safest, and add one thing you would watch for.

### Grown-up note

Practice is planning and revising, on paper. No real water is required. Switching is not failing the first idea.

### Writing prompt

Which path would you pick, and what would you watch for?

## Sprout — The Medal for Slow

**Heartlight:** Traillight  
**Id:** `medal-for-slow`

> I almost sprinted. The riddle wants the careful second attempt.

The moss gate asks which footprint is the thoughtful one. What do you think?

### Choose a strategy

- **The fastest print.** Speed is a guess here. The gate does not open for it — and that is all right.
- **The one that stopped to look.** A pause left a deeper print beside a tiny trail mark.
- **The one that turned around.** Going back, then trying again, is still a footprint.

### Reveal the explanation

The gate opens for the print that turned around. Sprout pins the medal on that second attempt. Fast was not wrong for every day. Today the riddle was about noticing. Traillight makes the medal glow emerald, then fades so nobody has to wear it.

### Try this next

Try a tiny task twice: once in a rush, once slower. Name one thing only the slower try noticed.

### Grown-up note

The story values a second look. Don’t turn it into “fast is bad.” Some things are fine done quickly. Notice what the child actually spotted.

### Writing prompt

What did a slower try notice?

## Luna — Weather Inside

**Heartlight:** Dreamlight  
**Id:** `weather-inside`

> You don’t have to explain the sky. I can sit in it with you.

A friend’s feeling is stormy, and they do not want to say why. What can Luna offer?

### Choose a strategy

- **Quiet company.** Sit nearby. No questions. Dreamlight stays low and steady.
- **A story they can change.** Offer a tale. They may change the ending, or close the book.
- **A drawing, no words.** Weather on paper. The reason can stay private.

### Reveal the explanation

None of these asks “why”. Luna does not demand an explanation for a feeling. If words come later, there is room. If they don’t, company was the whole help. Dreamlight does not dim when a feeling stays unnamed.

### Try this next

If you want, draw today’s feeling as weather. You may keep the reason to yourself. Blank paper is a finished drawing too.

### Grown-up note

Listening without requiring disclosure. If a child seems distressed, this page is not care — stay with them and use the support you already trust. It is not a treatment, and it does not reduce anxiety by itself.

### Writing prompt

A weather word for right now, if you want one. Blank is welcome.

## Luna — The Library of Almosts

**Heartlight:** Dreamlight  
**Id:** `library-of-almosts`

> An unfinished page is a door left kindly open.

The sentence waiting on the shelf is “And then the moon…” How might it go?

### Choose a strategy

- **…lent us a lantern.** A borrowed light. Very polite of the moon.
- **…waited while we thought.** No rush in the margin. The moon can hover.
- **…asked nothing and glowed.** Some lights do not need a speech.

### Reveal the explanation

All three endings are kind. Luna used to hide unfinished stories because they felt not-wonderful-enough. Dreamlight shows that a beginning can be the gift. Stopping is part of writing, not a hole in it.

### Try this next

Write one sentence and stop. You do not have to read it to anyone. You do not have to finish it tomorrow.

### Grown-up note

Lowers the pressure to perform a perfect ending. Anything typed in the box below stays in the page only — it is not saved.

### Writing prompt

Finish “And then the moon…” in your own way, or leave it almost.

## Luna — The Scarf Offer

**Heartlight:** Dreamlight  
**Id:** `scarf-offer`

> Words are an invitation, not a test.

Luna’s comet scarf can hold a feeling-word. Do you want to offer one?

### Choose a strategy

- **I’ll pick a word.** Any word. Luna does not grade it or ask you to explain it.
- **Not today.** A complete answer. Luna nods and lets the scarf rest.
- **A colour instead.** Violet, moss, gold, moon-blue, rose. A colour can be the whole reply.

### Reveal the explanation

“Not today” does not need a follow-up question. Luna does not chase a feeling to make the story neat. Dreamlight stays soft either way. An invitation can be declined and still have been kind.

### Try this next

If you like, match three feelings to three colours. Skip any you would rather not name.

### Grown-up note

Consent about sharing inner life. After this story, don’t quiz a child to “use their words.” Silence can be the respectful ending.

### Writing prompt

A word, a colour, or nothing. The box does not mind.

## Nori — Lowbeam’s Offer

**Heartlight:** Tidelight  
**Id:** `lowbeam-offer`

> We could notice three quiet things. And it might not help. That’s all right.

Nori offers a quiet lantern activity. What do you want?

### Choose a strategy

- **Yes — three quiet things.** One you can see, one you can hear, one you can touch. Stop if you want.
- **No — it doesn’t feel helpful.** Nori accepts that. Tidelight does not droop. The offer is over.
- **Something else.** A sip of water, a stretch, or just sitting. Nori will follow your lead.

### Reveal the explanation

A calming activity is an offer, not a rule. Nori used to think Lowbeam should always soothe. Some nights it doesn’t, and the friendship still holds. You can say no without owing a better idea.

### Try this next

Only if you want: name one thing you see, one you hear, and one you can touch. If that feels wrong, stop. Stopping is the activity working.

### Grown-up note

Shows that a regulation idea is optional. Do not insist on breathing or noticing games. If worry is large, use real support — this is fiction, not a way to shrink anxiety.

### Writing prompt

If you tried it: did it help, a little, or not at all? Every answer is allowed.

## Nori — Three Small Things

**Heartlight:** Tidelight  
**Id:** `three-small-things`

> That pebble has a stripe like a tiny river. What else do you notice?

The tidepool is full of tiny wonders. How do we look?

### Choose a strategy

- **A stripe or a mark.** Pick one pattern. You do not have to catalogue the whole pool.
- **The quietest sound.** A drip, a sleeve, a far bird. Name one, then rest.
- **One question, not ten.** Wonder about a single thing. Let the other questions wait.

### Reveal the explanation

Nori once rehearsed a question so carefully that a beetle walked away. Tidelight prefers a small wonder, spoken in time. Three details, then one question, is a complete adventure.

### Try this next

With a grown-up if you leave the room, notice three tiny details on one cup, leaf or stone. Wonder about only one of them.

### Grown-up note

Attention without a quiz at the end. Don’t ask them to report all three unless they offer. Curiosity here is shared, not marked.

### Writing prompt

One small thing you noticed, and one question about it.

## Nori — The Trusted Door

**Heartlight:** Tidelight  
**Id:** `trusted-door`

> Some questions are too big for a lantern. We can practise asking a person.

Lowbeam cannot answer this one. Who could?

### Choose a strategy

- **A grown-up at home.** Nori helps with the sentence, not with the secret.
- **A teacher or another adult you trust.** You choose the person. The story does not pick them for you.
- **I’ll think, and not write a name.** Deciding privately still counts. The page does not need the name.

### Reveal the explanation

A sentence you can borrow: “Can I tell you something that’s been sitting with me?” Nori does not ask what the something is. Heartlights are not people, and they are not a place for secrets. Asking someone you trust is the step that matters.

### Try this next

Practise the sentence with a toy if you want. Later, if it feels right, say it to a person you trust. You choose whether that happens.

## Moki — After the Drumroll

**Heartlight:** Sparklight  
**Id:** `after-the-drumroll`

> I tripped on my own entrance. I’m sorry. I’ll give you a little space.

Moki apologised for the crash. What would feel okay?

### Choose a strategy

- **A bit of space.** Moki steps back and keeps the confetti in a pocket. The joke waits.
- **A small laugh later.** Not now. Later, if you want it. Moki does not schedule the laugh.
- **I accept — let’s fix the mess.** Pick up what fell. Fixing is allowed to be quiet.

### Reveal the explanation

The apology comes before the joke. Space is a real choice, not a trick to hurry forgiveness. Sparklight stays in the room without crowding. Moki used to fill every silence. Today the silence is part of being kind.

### Try this next

Practise a short sorry you might actually use: what happened, that you are sorry, and one way to help. Then wait. Don’t add a joke unless the other person does.

### Grown-up note

Models repair plus room. Don’t ask a child to forgive on a timer, and don’t turn the story into a script they must perform after every bump.

### Writing prompt

What would “a little space” look like for you? You can skip this.

## Moki — Captain Teaspoon

**Heartlight:** Sparklight  
**Id:** `captain-teaspoon`

> Captain Teaspoon says triangles have four sides. Shall we check the captain’s maths?

How do we check a claim that sounds funny?

### Choose a strategy

- **Count the sides.** Draw one and touch each side as you count. The drawing is the evidence.
- **Ask what the word means.** “Tri” means three. The word itself is a clue, then the drawing checks it.
- **Compare with four sides.** Draw a square too. Set them side by side. Different shapes, different counts.

### Reveal the explanation

A triangle has three sides. Captain Teaspoon mixed it up with a square, which has four. The wrong guess was not embarrassing once it was checked. Oops. Plot twist. Sparklight likes the moment the idea gets corrected, kindly.

### Try this next

Draw a triangle and a square. Count the sides out loud. Then invent one wrong-on-purpose fact and correct it together.

### Grown-up note

Separates a guess from a checked idea, in play. It is not a geometry test and does not say anything about a child’s school results.

### Writing prompt

One wrong-on-purpose fact, then the checked fact.

## Moki — The Moon Behind the Cloud

**Heartlight:** Sparklight  
**Id:** `moon-behind-cloud`

> I announced the moon was missing. Then I looked up properly.

Moki says the moon is gone. What should we do before we agree?

### Choose a strategy

- **Wait and watch the cloud.** Clouds move. A minute of looking is a fair test.
- **Ask what clouds do.** Nori has watched this before. A friend can be a source, then you still look.
- **Check the other clues.** Stars are still there. The sky did not pack up. Something may be in the way.

### Reveal the explanation

The moon was behind a cloud. Moki’s announcement was confident and unfinished. Evidence was the sky, watched for a little while. Sparklight and Tidelight check together. A missing moon did not need a rescue — it needed a second look.

### Try this next

Next time something seems to have vanished, list two other explanations before you decide it is gone. A sock, a pencil, a moon.

### Grown-up note

Guess versus evidence, told as fiction. Enjoy the joke. Don’t turn every mix-up at home into a lesson.

### Writing prompt

What else could explain a “missing” moon?
